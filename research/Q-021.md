# Research question — Q-021

*Drafted by the Chief of Staff; checked by the Critic for gaps and neutral phrasing (D-032, D-055).*

## The question

What is known about errors in summaries written by machines, how they are caught, and how readers rely on such summaries?

The groundwork map raised these as open (research/Q-016-memo.md, areas F1, F4, G1 to G4 and H1 to H3). Nothing in this question assumes that machine-written summaries help, harm, or will be used.

From published studies, benchmarks, practitioner research and vendor documentation (for example, on summarising documents, code changes or pull requests):
- how often machine-written summaries are wrong, leave out something important, or state things the source does not hold, and which kinds of input or task make errors more or less likely;
- what has been found to reduce or catch such errors (for example, a second reader, comparison with the source, quoting the source, sampling), at what cost, and whether an author checking its own summary differs from an independent check;
- how readers treat a summary according to what it says about its origin or checking (no mark, machine-written, unchecked, checked by someone else), and whether any such mark changes how much they rely on it or how closely they check;
- what is known about a summary that goes out of date when the thing it describes changes, and how readers notice;
- for each finding, how strong the evidence is and how close its setting is to one owner reading summaries of changes in several projects built mostly by AI agents.

Paywalled studies count only where an abstract or an open version can be read; say which was read. Grade vendor claims as vendor claims.

## The decision it serves

Whether any machine-written account of a change could be shown to the owner, whether and what checking it would need, and whether it should say anything about its own origin or checking. Which content may leave the repositories is not part of this question.

## What each answer would change

Measured error rates set what checking is needed before any such account is shown, or rule one out; checks found to work, and their cost, set what checking is feasible; evidence on how readers rely on marked or unmarked summaries sets whether a mark helps, does nothing, or makes things worse; weak or distant evidence leaves the choice open or to a cheap trial.
