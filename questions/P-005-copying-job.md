# Ruling needed: May each of your four repositories (Service-Desk, Model-Lab, the portfolio and the operating model) run a job that saves copies of GitHub's run and check results before GitHub deletes them, and how often?

Milestone: M7

WHY: You ruled that GitHub's run and check results are kept for the whole life of the work, as copies saved in each project's own repository (your ruling "B: Copies as repo files"). Nothing is copied until you approve the job that does it. GitHub's pages say that from 1 October 2026 it deletes these results once they pass each repository's setting, 90 days by default; that the deleting has started rests on one GitHub page, and a copy can only be made while GitHub still holds the original. On the two public repositories nothing should have been lost yet, if their setting is still the default 90 days: Service-Desk was created on 30 September 2026 and its oldest automated run is from that day, and Model-Lab was created on 1 November, as the Chief of Staff saw on GitHub on 13 November. At 90 days, Service-Desk's first results would be deleted from about 29 December 2026. Their settings are not checked; Service-Desk's oldest run still being there on 13 November means its setting is at least about 44 days. You can see each setting in the repository's Actions settings. The two private repositories, the portfolio and the operating model, the Chief of Staff cannot see; you can see their oldest runs and their setting in each one's Actions settings. The research on how such a job works and what it costs has passed its source check, so the job can now be put to you. Approving can be undone: the job can be removed later, and the copies stay.
How to answer: one letter. Choosing A or B also approves its small new spend (see Background).
If you don't answer: nothing is copied, though waiting is not the same as C, since A, B or D stay open; on the public two nothing should be lost before about late December if their setting is 90 days, and on the private two it depends on their age and setting. The card does not default (see RISK CLASS). Other work on the timeline goes on.

Background (a "sweep" is one pass of the job over everything GitHub still holds, copying what is new):
- In the research's opinion, one job per repository can copy all three kinds of result: automated runs, checks, and commit statuses (the pass-or-fail marks on each change). It would use the key GitHub gives each job automatically, which reaches only that repository and expires within hours, with read access to runs, checks and statuses and write access to the repository's files. No new key is made, and the desk's key stays read-only. A sweep makes about two requests to GitHub per commit it looks at, plus one per hundred runs and its writes, and must stay under 1,000 requests an hour; whether that limit is shared with the repository's other jobs, the Orchestrator's included, is not known, and a first catch-up over 90 days of history may come near it.
- No single GitHub event sees all three kinds, and GitHub's scheduled starts can be late or dropped, so in the research's opinion the job should sweep on a schedule, and when started by hand, and catch up on its next run. It can catch up only on results GitHub still holds.
- GitHub switches off a public repository's scheduled job after 60 days with no activity in it. Whether the job's own saves count as activity is not known. Service-Desk and Model-Lab are the public two. If the job stopped there, copying would stop with nothing visible unless something outside it checks; the plan's rules require a check from outside any feed that shows it as stopped, and how this job is checked is set in Design.
- The copies go on a branch of their own in each repository, which no other job writes to, so they should not collide with the Orchestrator's commits or be held by your pull-request rules, unless a repository has a rule that names or matches every branch; no record yet says who checks this, and the Chief of Staff would add it to Design's checks. The desk reads that branch with its read-only key, one request per file.
- What a copy keeps, by kind: a run keeps its name, outcome, who started it and when it was created, started and last updated, but no end time; a check keeps its name, outcome, start and end times and the app that made it, but not who started it; a status keeps its state, who posted it and when, but no start time. No copy keeps logs, annotations or artifacts: the Chief of Staff proposes leaving them out, since logs can hold private detail and two repositories are public. Links to GitHub's own page for a run stop working once GitHub deletes it.
- On the two private repositories the desk's read-only key cannot read check results at all, so there the copies would be the timeline's only source of them: under a daily sweep a check result there shows up to about a day late.
- Cost, by the research's figures: free on the public repositories. On the private ones each run is billed as at least one whole minute; a daily run on GitHub's smallest machine, at $0.002 a minute, comes to about 30 minutes, about 6 cents, a month per repository, if each sweep ends within a minute (an estimate; a first catch-up over 90 days of history may take longer, and a job on that machine is stopped after 15 minutes).
- What that means for your account: the desk's spec records that your account is on Pro, with 3,000 included minutes a month, has a card on file, and was past its included minutes in October, so further minutes were charged; this month's figure is on GitHub's billing page. If it still holds, the job is new spend: about 12 cents a month for the two private repositories under A, about $2.88 under B. The plan's ceiling is $0 of new spend unless you approve more, so choosing A or B also approves that spend.

OPTIONS:
  A. A daily sweep — each repository runs the job once a day, and when started by hand, on GitHub's smallest machine. Gain: every result still held is copied within about a day, at about 6 cents a month per private repository (an estimate), and a missed day is caught up on the next; while a repository keeps results for more than a few days, a daily sweep saves everything an hourly one would. Cost: on the private two, check results reach the timeline up to about a day late; four new automated jobs and four new branches, a change to what your projects record, made in the operating model and copied into each project. Results already deleted cannot be recovered.
  B. An hourly sweep — the same job, run every hour. Gain: copies are at most about an hour old, at no cost on the public two, and a late or dropped start is caught up on the next run, within the hour; it saves nothing a daily sweep would lose, but on the private two check results reach the timeline within about an hour rather than a day. Cost: at least about 720 minutes, about $1.44, a month per private repository (an estimate, if each sweep ends within a minute), $2.88 for the two, new spend if your account is still past its included minutes; and up to 24 saves a day to each repository's copy branch.
  D. A on the public two only — the daily job on Service-Desk and Model-Lab, none on the private two. Gain: no new spend, so it keeps the plan's $0 ceiling. Cost: the private two's results are not copied, and their check results never reach the timeline, since the read key cannot see them; the private two can be added later by a new ruling.
  C. Not now — no job. Gain: nothing changes in your repositories, and nothing is spent. Cost: it goes against your ruling to keep these results for the whole life of the work: each result GitHub deletes shows on the timeline only as "no longer kept", and is lost for good.
RECOMMENDATION: A, with moderate confidence — of the options offered that carry out your ruling, it costs least, about 12 cents a month, and loses nothing an hourly sweep would save. What would change it: if you want the private repositories' check results on the timeline within the hour, B; if you would rather keep to $0 of new spend, D.
RISK CLASS: R3 here (it adds an automated job to each repository's workflow files, which this repository's governance/risk-paths.toml makes gate-bearing, needing your approval; the other three repositories' own rules are applied when each adopts it)   REVERSIBILITY: reversible — removing the job later stops the copying and the copies stay; a result deleted before a copy is made cannot be recovered under any option; as an R3 change it needs your approval   BLAST RADIUS: your four repositories, each gaining one job and one branch; about 12 cents a month (A) or $2.88 (B) of charged Actions minutes on the private two; nothing on the desk changes until the timeline is built
DEFAULT: none — an R3 change needs your approval and never defaults   TIMEOUT: none

Not offered: a weekly sweep, which would cost even less but leave private check results up to a week late; a job started by each run as it ends, which in the research's worked example (40 runs a day; real volumes are not known) costs more on the private repositories than a daily sweep and still misses checks and statuses, so it would need the sweep anyway; copies on the main branch, which would race the Orchestrator's commits and be blocked by a rule requiring pull requests unless the job could bypass it; copying logs, a separate decision with its own cost, since logs can hold private detail.

Also open to you, whichever letter you choose: your two private repositories can be set to keep results for up to 400 days rather than 90, in each repository's settings; whether a longer setting uses any of GitHub's free storage allowance is not researched. Raising it now keeps more results for a later copy, since changing the setting does not bring back what is already deleted. The public two cannot go past 90.

Checked by: the Critic, reading this card cold in fresh sessions on a different model from the writer's; its rounds are to be listed in the pull request that files the card.

Not settled here: how long copies are kept, and the exact file layout, both set in Design; the job itself is built in the operating model and reviewed before any repository adopts it.
