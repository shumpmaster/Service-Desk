# Research question — Q-016

*Drafted by the Chief of Staff; checked by the Critic for gaps and neutral phrasing (D-032, D-055).*

## The question

Groundwork map: how one owner who oversees several projects, worked on mostly by AI agents, keeps up with the completed and waiting changes in them.

Changes in this setup mostly arrive as a pull request on GitHub, from several project repositories and from one portfolio repository that spans them. Pull-request descriptions in this setup are required to follow a fixed form: a plain-English paragraph headed "What this means for you", a line naming the milestone it serves, a "Context" section of facts each with its source, a "Why" section, and a "Tasks" section of tick boxes (shumpmaster/Personal-Org-Operating-Model, OPERATING_MODEL.md §2 "Plain English first", S-026; §3 "Why and context", S-032, and "Tasks and steps", S-034). CI checks the form in v3 projects, not the content. Older pull requests were not rewritten, and nothing in this question assumes the descriptions are accurate or complete. The owner already has a status page, the desk (shumpmaster/Service-Desk; what it shows is described in docs/PROJECT.md and docs/handover/); its pull-request rows come from the projects' repositories, and the portfolio repository is read for its records. The owner reads mostly on a phone.

Ways of keeping up that are in view include: reading pull requests on GitHub as now; the desk as it is; a per-change summary shown somewhere; a periodic digest; notifications only; and doing nothing new. The map should cover all of them.

Map the ground an expert would cover before deciding whether the owner needs anything beyond what exists, and if so what, for whom and in what form:
- how the owner keeps up with changes today, what that costs in time and what gets missed, and what a no-change option would leave unsolved;
- what the owner needs to learn about a change, and when: on its own, beside others, before or after it merges, and how this differs for a change waiting on the owner;
- what it means for the owner to have seen or dealt with a change (read, acknowledged, acted on, cleared), whether that is recorded, and what the reader does next;
- how many changes arrive per day or week across the projects, and how that affects what is readable, what is skipped, and what any approach costs;
- for any per-change summary: what it can hold and still be read at a glance, and which parts of a pull request (its description, its diff, its checks, its reviews, its linked records) matter for that;
- how summaries could be produced (taken from what the author wrote, generated from the change, written by a person, or a mix), and what each means for accuracy, for incomplete or wrong descriptions, and for who checks the summary;
- how a summary can mislead or be wrong (an omitted risk, a flattering account of the author's own work, a stale or missing description), how that would be noticed, what the owner relies on it for, and what happens when it fails;
- what happens to a summary when the change moves on after it was written (new commits, an edited description, review findings, checks turning red, a merge or close), and how the reader knows which state it describes;
- what data would leave the repositories if anything is generated, to whom, under what terms, and whether the repositories' privacy and the owner's accounts allow it;
- how a set of changes is ordered, grouped, filtered and navigated (by project, milestone, time, kind or state);
- how any approach relates to what the desk already shows and to GitHub: what it replaces, duplicates or adds;
- the owner's time to use it, the agents' cost to produce and keep it, and how its value could be measured or tried cheaply;
- whether the form pull requests are written in would need to change, and what that would cost the projects;
- whether the same applies to changes that are not pull requests (direct commits, deploys, records);
- reading on a small screen: length, layout, images and charts, and accessibility;
- what other fields do to brief one decision-maker on many changes or events (for example, change logs, release notes, intelligence or executive briefings, status reports), including where such briefing was found to mislead, to be skipped, or to add little.

## The decision it serves

The shape of the Discovery for how the owner keeps up with changes across projects: which questions go to the owner, which go to research, and which need an experiment, before its value target, scope and plan are set.

## What each answer would change

Each area found to be unknown becomes a research request or a registered experiment; each found to be the owner's call goes on the owner's shortlist; each design trap found shapes the scope.
